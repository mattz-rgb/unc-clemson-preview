ACC Football • Week 3

UNC vs. Clemson

ACC Opener Preview
Saturday, Sept. 19 Noon ET Memorial Stadium ESPN

Saturday, Sept. 19, Noon ET, Memorial Stadium (ESPN)

Bill Belichick's North Carolina Tar Heels head into Death Valley at 2-0 for the first time in his tenure, but the story of the opening two weeks has been the defence rather than the offence.

UNC has allowed just 13 points through two games, hasn't given up more than 180 passing yards in either contest and has held both TCU and East Tennessee State below three yards per carry. The competition has been modest outside of TCU, but the Tar Heels have been disciplined, physical and particularly good against the run.

The offence has been more workmanlike. UNC ranks 11th in the ACC in scoring, 13th in total offence, ninth in passing and 12th in rushing. The Tar Heels are averaging 25 points and 374.5 yards per game, so this isn't an offence that has been overwhelming opponents.

That sets up an interesting ACC opener against a Clemson team that has had almost the exact opposite experience. The Tigers have some legitimate talent on defence and along the edge of their defensive line, but their offence has been one of the least efficient units in the country through two games.

For UNC, the formula is fairly straightforward: run at Clemson's interior defensive line, move Jelani Thurman around the formation, protect Billy Edwards Jr. against Clemson's edge rushers and let the defence put pressure on a true freshman quarterback.

For Clemson, the challenge is much harder. The Tigers need to find a way to run the football, protect Tait Reynolds and generate enough explosive plays to stop UNC from turning the game into a defensive grind.

UNC's offence

Billy Edwards Jr has been steady through two games.

The sixth-year Wisconsin transfer is 31 of 48 passing for 476 yards, two touchdowns and zero interceptions. He threw for 232 yards in UNC's 15-10 win over TCU in Dublin before completing 15-of-20 passes for 244 yards and two touchdowns against ETSU.

Edwards has done a good job spreading the ball around. Eleven different players caught a pass against ETSU, and UNC has enough weapons that opponents can't simply focus on one receiver.

The concern has been the deep passing game. Edwards has missed a couple of opportunities for explosive plays, including an early overthrow to Jordan Shipp against TCU. Against Clemson, UNC will probably need to convert some of those opportunities if it wants to avoid having every possession turn into a field-position battle.

The running game is led by Demon June, who has emerged as one of the most exciting players on the roster. June scored three rushing touchdowns against ETSU and flashed his ability as a freshman, when he rushed for 464 yards on 84 carries at 5.5 yards per carry while adding 17 catches for 159 yards.

He has genuine RB1 talent, but that doesn't mean UNC needs to turn him into a 25 carry a game back. This should still be a rotation.

Benjamin Hall and Jaylen McGill should remain involved, with the exact distribution likely changing depending on game situation, down and distance and how well Clemson is defending the run. June can be the best back in the rotation without having to monopolise it.

That could actually work in UNC's favour against Clemson's front. If the Tar Heels can establish the run and consistently rotate fresh backs, they can keep attacking an interior defensive line that has shown a tendency to get moved out of its gaps.

Attack Clemson's interior

This is one of the clearest areas where UNC should have an advantage to exploit. Clemson's interior defensive line has been susceptible to being pushed laterally and out of the run lane. UNC shouldn't need June to create everything himself if the offensive line can consistently get movement at the point of attack.

That's particularly important because UNC's overall rushing numbers haven't been great. The Tar Heels are averaging just 125.5 rushing yards per game, ranking 106th nationally. This isn't necessarily a situation where UNC suddenly becomes a dominant rushing offence. It's about finding a specific weakness and attacking it repeatedly. If the Tar Heels can move Clemson's interior defenders, June, Hall and McGill can all get downhill with cleaner lanes.

Thurman vs. Jeremiah Alexander

One of the more interesting individual matchups is Jelani Thurman against Clemson linebacker Jeremiah Alexander.

Alexander is one of Clemson's better defensive players, and UNC shouldn't make his job easy by allowing him to sit in one spot and play downhill all afternoon. Thurman should be moved around.

Line him up attached to the formation. Flex him into the slot. Motion him across the formation. Put him in different alignments and make Alexander and Clemson's secondary communicate. The point isn't necessarily to force Alexander into coverage on every play. It's to make him constantly process where Thurman is and what the formation means before the snap.

Thurman has already shown what kind of impact he can have. He caught two passes for 55 yards on UNC's opening drive against TCU, and his size and athleticism give Edwards a legitimate mismatch over the middle of the field.

If UNC can make Alexander hesitate, it creates opportunities elsewhere. A linebacker occupied by Thurman is one less player immediately attacking the run. A safety rotating towards Thurman potentially opens something for Shipp or another receiver.

UNC should make Clemson defend Thurman rather than simply letting Clemson decide how it wants to defend him.

UNC's receivers

Jordan Shipp remains the veteran security blanket, while Trech Kekahuna brings another explosive element to the passing game. Kekahuna, a Wisconsin transfer who played with Edwards, has plenty of ability but has had some ball-security issues after fumbling against TCU. Jelani Thurman has already shown he can be a major factor, while Mason Humphrey has been quieter than UNC would probably like. Humphrey had just one catch for 10 yards against ETSU.

That becomes more important because UNC will be without freshman receiver CJ Sadler.

Sadler has been one of UNC's more explosive receivers through the first two games, with four catches for 73 yards overall and three catches for 66 yards against ETSU. He is unavailable for Clemson following a traffic-related legal matter and is listed as out on UNC's availability report. The Tar Heels therefore need Shipp, Kekahuna, Thurman and Humphrey to absorb more of the passing-game workload. A name to watch in this room is true freshman Jaden Jefferson, a small slot wide receiver with blazing speed and fantastic footwork, as he may very well take a few snaps to spell Trech Kekahuna.

UNC's defence and the Melkart Abou-Jaoude situation

This is the headline matchup. Melkart Abou-Jaoude, the reigning ACC sack leader after recording 10.5 sacks in 2025, remains questionable after suffering a hand injury against TCU. He played through the injury in Dublin, finishing with four tackles, a sack, two pressures and a forced fumble, but then sat out the ETSU game.

He is now expected to play against Clemson with his hand wrapped, although the expectation is that UNC will manage his workload rather than simply giving him every defensive snap. He remains officially questionable on the ACC availability report.

That changes how UNC should use him.

If Abou-Jaoude is limited, his highest-value snaps are likely to come on obvious passing downs, third downs and other situations where UNC knows Clemson has to throw. UNC doesn't necessarily need him for 60-70 snaps. It needs him to be effective when he is on the field. And there is a very obvious matchup UNC can attack.

Attack Clemson's tackles

Clemson's offensive line is going to have a major test against UNC's pass rush. The Tigers offensive line has not been able to consistently protect the quarterback or create clean running lanes. Collin Sadler is the Clemson offensive lineman to watch here. He's a 6-foot-6, 310-pound veteran who has played both guard and tackle during his Clemson career, giving the Tigers some flexibility with their line combinations.

If Collin Sadler is the player UNC gets isolated against on the right side, the Tar Heels should be willing to attack that matchup. Melkart Abou-Jaoude, Harvey and Lewis can all be moved around the formation to prevent Clemson from settling into comfortable protection calls.

The objective should be to make Clemson's tackles deal with different rushers and different angles throughout the game. UNC doesn't want Abou-Jaoude sitting in one predictable alignment where Clemson can simply slide protection towards him. The hand injury makes that even more important. If Abou-Jaoude is playing limited snaps, UNC needs to maximise the situations in which he is on the field.

Clemson's pass rush vs. UNC's tackles

The matchup goes both ways. UNC's offensive tackles are also going to be tested because Clemson's edge rushers are legitimately good, especially Will Heldt, their star pass rusher. The fact that Clemson's offence has struggled doesn't mean its defensive front is easy to handle. UNC cannot afford to let Clemson's edge players consistently win one-on-one against its tackles.

The difference is what happens inside. Clemson has struggled to generate interior pressure, which could make life easier for UNC if its tackles can hold up against the edge rushers. The three man rotation of Andy Burburija, Devarrick Woods and Amare Adams have all struggled both against the run and especially causing havoc for the opposing offensive line. If Clemson can win on the outside but UNC keeps the middle of the pocket clean, Edwards should have enough room to step up and find Thurman, Shipp and the rest of the passing game.

Former 5 star defensive tackle, Vic Burley, returns from suspension, although he is not listed within Clemson’s depth chart, and does not project to have much impact on the game, even if he plays a high percentage of snaps.

If Clemson starts getting pressure from both the edges and the interior, however, the game changes quickly. That's why the offensive line matchup could be one of the most important aspects of Saturday.

Clemson's offence is in serious trouble

Through two games against LSU and Georgia Southern, Clemson's offence has been among the least efficient in the country.

The Tigers have produced only 37 total yards before contact on designed runs, ranking 130th nationally. They're averaging 3.3 yards per rush, ranking 132nd, and have yet to convert a third down on the ground. More than a third of Clemson's runs have been stopped for either no gain or a loss. Their 38% rate ranks 138th nationally.

The passing game isn't much better. Clemson is averaging just 4.6 yards per dropback, ranking 130th. Its 8.7% sack rate ranks 124th, while its overall successful-play rate is only 32%, ranking 137th. Those numbers explain the 16 points per game.

Clemson isn't just struggling to finish drives. It is struggling to consistently produce positive plays in the first place. The Tigers aren't getting enough yards before contact. They're not consistently winning on the ground. They're not converting third downs with the run. They're allowing too much pressure. And now they are turning to a true freshman quarterback.

Tait Reynolds gets the start

Christopher Vizzina will not play against UNC after being listed as probably due to suffering an elbow injury against Georgia Southern, and being benched in favour of freshman Tait Reynolds , who will make his first career start.

Reynolds came into the Georgia Southern game after Vizzina was injured and completed 16 of 27 passes for 178 yards. He also saw action in Clemson's season-opening loss to LSU. Through two games, he has completed 60.6% of his passes, but he is still looking for his first career touchdown pass.

The encouraging part for Clemson is that Reynolds brings mobility. That gives UNC something slightly different to prepare for and creates the possibility of extending plays that otherwise might become sacks. The problem is that mobility only helps if Clemson can give him manageable situations. If Clemson is constantly facing second-and-9 or third-and-8 because the running game is being stuffed, UNC can attack Reynolds with its pass rush. That's exactly the situation the Tar Heels want.

Clemson's skill players

T.J. Moore has been Clemson's leading receiver early in the season with 134 yards. Bryant Wesco Jr. is the bigger explosive-play threat, though. Wesco has averaged 17.3 yards per reception in each of the last two seasons and has the size and speed to turn relatively ordinary plays into chunk gains. Both are NFL level talents, (Moore is WR7, Wesco is WR11 on my draft rankings), although they have not been able to make much of an impact this year due to subpar offensive line and quarterback play. Rounding the room up, Tyler Brown has 55 receiving yards, while Juju Preston has 25.

For Reynolds, the priority should be getting the ball to these players without asking him to repeatedly hold the ball and wait for deep routes to develop. Clemson needs easy completions. It needs quick game. It needs to create manageable down-and-distance situations, and most importantly, it needs to find a way to get its running game moving.

UNC's defensive numbers

UNC's defensive statistics are significantly better. The Tar Heels are allowing just 91.5 rushing yards per game and 2.47 yards per carry, with the latter ranking 21st nationally. They're also allowing only 6.5 points per game, tied for fifth nationally. The seven sacks rank 11th nationally.

The pass defence hasn't been quite as dominant, with 353 passing yards allowed through two games, but UNC has still done a good job preventing opponents from consistently producing points. There is one obvious weakness: third-down defence.

Opponents have converted 46.9% of their third downs against UNC, which ranks 121st nationally.

So while the scoring and rushing numbers look excellent, the defence hasn't been completely dominant on every down.

Clemson's inability to run the ball could help UNC, though. If the Tigers continue struggling on early downs, UNC can force Reynolds into the passing situations where its pass rush becomes most dangerous.

UNC's offence has plenty of questions too

It's important not to pretend UNC is some offensive juggernaut. The Tar Heels rank 106th nationally in rushing, 90th in total offence and tied for 93rd in scoring. Their third-down conversion rate is only 40%, and they are averaging just 26 minutes of possession per game. There is, however, an interesting number in the passing game.

UNC is averaging 14.65 yards per completion, ranking 24th nationally.

That fits the way the offence has looked. UNC isn't dominating with volume. It's capable of producing explosive gains when Edwards connects with his receivers. The red-zone numbers are also interesting. UNC has scored on six of seven trips inside the red zone, but all four touchdowns have come on the ground. The other two scores were field goals.

That could matter against Clemson. If the Tigers can force UNC into field goals instead of touchdowns, they can keep a low-scoring game within reach, especially if kicker Rece Verhoff is not granted an exemption to play.

The game plan for UNC

There are a few things I'd expect UNC to emphasise. First, attack Clemson's interior defensive line. If Clemson is susceptible to getting pushed out of its gaps, UNC should run directly at that weakness rather than constantly trying to create everything outside.

Second, move Jelani Thurman around. Don't allow Jeremiah Alexander to play a comfortable game. Make Clemson communicate. Force Alexander to identify where Thurman is before the snap and make the Tigers adjust their coverage and run fits.

Third, attack Clemson's tackles. If Collin Sadler ends up at right tackle, UNC should test that side with its best pass rushers. Even if Melkart is limited, Harvey and Lewis can be moved around to create favourable matchups.

And finally, force Tait Reynolds to beat the defence from the pocket. The freshman is athletic enough to create problems when plays break down, so UNC shouldn't give him easy escape lanes. Keep him contained, make him work through progressions and force Clemson to sustain long drives.

The game plan for Clemson

Clemson has to do almost the opposite.

The Tigers need to avoid third-and-long, which starts with the running game. If Clemson continues producing almost nothing before contact, UNC will eventually pin its ears back and attack Reynolds.

The Tigers also need to find a way to protect Reynolds without committing every resource to the pass rush. That is where Bryant Wesco Jr. and T.J. Moore become important. Clemson needs quick completions and explosive plays without asking Reynolds to sit in the pocket for four seconds.

The worst-case scenario is an offence that can't run the ball, can't protect its quarterback and therefore becomes completely predictable on third down, and UNC certainly has enough pass rushing talent to punish that.

The prediction

Neither offence has been particularly efficient, but Clemson's offensive problems are much more extensive. The Tigers have struggled to run the ball, protect the quarterback and consistently create successful plays, and now they are putting a true freshman into his first start against a UNC defence that has already recorded seven sacks.

UNC has its own problems. The loss of CJ Sadler removes an explosive receiver from an already inconsistent passing attack, and Clemson's edge rushers could make life difficult for the Tar Heels' tackles.

But UNC has a much clearer path to controlling the game. Run at Clemson's vulnerable interior. Rotate June, Hall and McGill. Move Thurman around and force Jeremiah Alexander to constantly adjust. Protect Edwards against Clemson's edge rushers. Then let the defensive front attack Reynolds.

The biggest swing factor is Melkart Abou-Jaoude. If he can play meaningful snaps despite the hand injury, even if UNC manages his workload, he gives the Tar Heels another player Clemson has to account for. He doesn't necessarily need to play every snap to have an impact, and if UNC's other pass rushers can win their matchups against Clemson's tackles, the Tigers could spend most of the afternoon in obvious passing situations.

For Clemson, the formula is much simpler: run the ball better, protect Reynolds, hit Wesco and Moore for some explosives and avoid putting the freshman in third-and-long all afternoon. If Clemson can do that, this becomes a much more competitive game. If it can't, UNC's defence should have enough control over the matchup to keep the Tigers' offence in the teens.

The numbers point towards a defensive, field-position-driven ACC opener. The individual matchups make it even more interesting: Thurman against Alexander, UNC's tackles against Clemson's edge rushers, Clemson's tackles against UNC's pass rush, and the health-managed Abou-Jaoude against an offensive line that has struggled to protect its quarterback.

For UNC, the game is there to be won at the line of scrimmage. For Clemson, the challenge is finding a way to stop that from happening.

Matt’s pick: UNC 20 – Clemson 16
